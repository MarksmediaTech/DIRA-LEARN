# DIRA LEARN V13.2 curriculum – Digital Marketing

Revised 19 Nov 2026. Status: **reviewed draft lesson plans (V13.2), authorised for approval by programme management.** Durations are the approved timetable and were not changed.

**Track focus.** Reach and win customers online. Learners build a business's online presence, understand their audience, plan and design content, write persuasive copy, use WhatsApp Business, search and simple advertising, measure results, and finish by building and presenting a basic digital marketing campaign for a real Kenyan business, cause or their own idea.

**Final project.** A basic digital marketing campaign for a real brief (a local business, youth enterprise, community organisation or the learner's own idea). It contains a SMART objective, a customer persona, a channel plan, a brand mini-kit, a two-week content calendar, at least three finished posts with captions, a WhatsApp Business plan, a small KES budget, the measures of success and a short results or test report, presented in session 8. Privacy option: a learner who cannot or does not wish to publish publicly may run the campaign as a private test (peers, or a small WhatsApp group whose members agreed to take part) or build it for the simulated brief provided by the facilitator, with mock-up posts and clearly labelled estimated results; it is assessed on the same rubric. A real business's name, logo, photos, prices or customer information are used only with the owner's written permission.

## Session 3 (Week 2, 120 minutes): Digital marketing foundations, online presence and reputation

*How digital marketing works, the main channels and strategies, digital footprint and online reputation, and building a strong personal and business presence. Learners choose their campaign brief.*

### 1. Learning purpose
Learners understand what digital marketing is, how customers move from first seeing a business to buying and returning, and which channels Kenyan small businesses use. They audit their own and a business's digital footprint, learn how reputation is built and repaired online, improve a profile, and choose the real business or cause they will build their campaign for.

### 2. Learning objectives
By the end of the session learners can:
- Explain digital marketing and the customer journey (awareness, consideration, conversion, loyalty) using a local business example
- Compare owned, earned and paid channels and name at least five digital channels used by Kenyan businesses
- Audit a digital footprint (personal and business) and identify three risks and three improvements
- Respond professionally to a negative online review
- Improve one personal or business profile for clarity, trust and contact
- Choose a campaign brief and draw a first channel map for it

### 3. Topics and subtopics
- **What digital marketing is**
    - Marketing versus selling
    - Digital versus traditional marketing (posters, radio, word of mouth)
    - Why small businesses in Kenya market on phones
    - What a campaign is
- **The customer journey**
    - Awareness
    - Consideration
    - Conversion
    - Loyalty and referral
    - Where each channel helps
- **Digital marketing channels and strategies**
    - Owned channels (profiles, WhatsApp Business, website, Google Business Profile)
    - Earned channels (shares, reviews, word of mouth, media)
    - Paid channels (boosted posts, ads, influencers)
    - Marketplaces (Jumia, Kilimall, Facebook Marketplace)
    - SMS and email
    - Choosing channels by audience and budget
- **Digital footprint and online reputation**
    - Active and passive footprint
    - What employers and customers find when they search you
    - Reviews and ratings
    - Responding to complaints
    - Protecting privacy and safety
- **Personal and business online presence**
    - Name, photo or logo, bio and contact consistency
    - Trust signals (reviews, real photos, location, opening hours, payment details)
    - The difference between a personal and a business account

### 5. Step-by-step teaching content
#### 0:00–0:10 · Welcome to Digital Marketing and track overview (10 min, introduction and recap)
*Meet your track group, see the six sessions and the final campaign you will build.*

1. Welcome learners to the track. Show the six-session journey on one slide: S3 foundations and presence, S4 audiences, social media and WhatsApp Business, S5 brand, content plan and graphics, S6 copy, conversion and search, S7 ads, budget, measurement and campaign build, S8 showcase.
2. Show a one-slide example of a finished campaign for a fictional business, 'Shiru's Bakes' (a home bakery in Ruiru selling cakes and bread on WhatsApp and Instagram): objective, persona, three posts, a KES 1,000 budget and results. Say: by session 8 you will have one like this for a real business.
3. Ask learners to share in the chat or aloud one business they buy from because of something they saw online.

#### 0:10–0:30 · What digital marketing is, the customer journey and channels (20 min, main lesson)
*Learn how customers move from noticing a business to buying again, and which channels help at each stage.*

1. Define it: digital marketing is using online channels - social media, WhatsApp, search, marketplaces, SMS and email - to help the right people find, trust, choose and return to a business. Selling is the moment of purchase; marketing is everything that makes that moment more likely.
2. Draw the customer journey with the Shiru's Bakes example. Awareness - a neighbour shares her cake photo on WhatsApp status. Consideration - the customer checks Instagram for prices and reviews. Conversion - orders on WhatsApp and pays with M-Pesa. Loyalty - gets a thank-you message and a birthday discount next year and refers a friend.
3. Explain owned, earned and paid channels with examples. Owned - the business's own Instagram, WhatsApp Business, Google Business Profile, website. Earned - reviews, shares, customers tagging the business, a local blog mention. Paid - boosted posts, Meta or Google ads, paying a micro-influencer. Most small Kenyan businesses start owned and earned, then add a little paid.
4. Tour the main channels quickly: Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn, WhatsApp Business, Google Search and Maps (Google Business Profile), marketplaces (Jumia, Kilimall, Facebook Marketplace), SMS and email. One line each on who uses it and what it is good for. Say that the next sessions go deeper into each.
5. Strategy in one sentence: right message, right people, right channel, right time, with a clear action. Ask: which channels would a mama mboga in Kawangware use, and which would a Nairobi graphic designer use? Why different?

#### 0:30–0:45 · Digital footprint and online reputation (15 min, main lesson)
*Understand what your online trail says about you or a business, and how reputation is protected and repaired.*

1. Explain the digital footprint. Active - what you post, comment, like and share. Passive - what others post about you, tags, reviews, location data, search results. Both stay for a long time and screenshots last forever.
2. Explain why it matters: employers and clients search candidates; customers read reviews before buying. A single rude reply to a complaint can travel further than ten good posts.
3. Reputation rules for a business: respond to every review (good or bad) within a day, thank publicly, solve privately, never argue or reveal a customer's personal details, fix the real problem, and ask happy customers for reviews.
4. Teach the review reply model ACE - Acknowledge ('Thank you for telling us, we are sorry your order was late'), Correct or explain briefly without excuses ('our rider had a breakdown'), Engage offline ('please WhatsApp us on our business number so we can make it right').
5. Privacy and safety: business accounts should use a business number, not a personal one; do not post customers' faces, children or home locations without consent; under Kenya's Data Protection Act 2019, customer data such as phone numbers must be collected and used fairly and for a clear purpose.

#### 0:45–1:00 · Demonstration - footprint audit and review reply (15 min, demonstration)
*Watch a live audit of a business's online presence and a professional reply to a bad review.*

1. Search a fictional or consenting local business name on Google and social media (prepare screenshots in case of poor connection). Score it with the presence checklist: name consistent, clear photo or logo, bio says what and where, contact and payment details, opening hours, recent posts, reviews, replies to reviews.
2. Show a fictional one-star review: 'Waited 2 hours for my cake and it was the wrong flavour. Never again.' Write the ACE reply live and read it aloud.
3. Show a bad reply ('You are lying, we delivered on time') and ask learners what damage it does.
4. Do a personal footprint search on yourself (prepared in advance) and show what you cleaned up: old public posts, a phone number in a bio, an unprofessional username.

#### 1:00–1:05 · Stretch break (5 min, stretch break)
*A five-minute stretch and movement break (seated option available).*

1. Invite everyone to stand, stretch, walk or drink water for five minutes; offer a seated option for anyone who prefers or needs it.
2. Restart on time with a one-line reminder of where the session is and what comes next.

#### 1:05–1:25 · Presence audit and profile improvement (20 min, practical exercise)
*Audit your own footprint and a business profile, then improve one profile.*

1. Part A (10 min, individual): learners search their own name and usernames. They list what they find, mark anything they would not want an employer or customer to see, and check privacy settings. Remind them not to share private findings with the group unless they want to.
2. Part B (15 min, individual): learners audit one business profile (their own business, a family business, or a business they will use as their campaign brief, with the owner's agreement) using the presence checklist, then write the improved version of the bio, the profile photo or logo choice, contact line and pinned post or highlight.
3. Give a bio formula: what you sell + who it is for + where + how to order. Example: 'Fresh cakes and bread for birthdays and offices in Ruiru and Juja. Order on WhatsApp 07XX XXX XXX. Pay via M-Pesa Till.'
4. Circulate. Check that learners use a business number and do not publish home addresses.

#### 1:25–1:45 · Choose your campaign brief and draw a channel map (20 min, group activity)
*Pick the real business or cause for your campaign and map which channels its customers use.*

1. Explain brief options: (a) a real local business with the owner's permission (a salon, cyber cafe, kinyozi, poultry farmer, fashion seller, food vendor); (b) a youth enterprise or community organisation (a youth recycling group, a church project, a sports club); (c) the learner's own business idea. The brief must be real enough to test with real people.
2. Each learner writes a one-paragraph brief: business name, what it sells or does, location, current customers, the problem (for example few new customers, quiet weekdays, low trust online), and one thing the owner wants in the next month.
3. In groups of three, learners take turns presenting their brief and drawing a channel map: list the channels, mark which ones the customers actually use, and choose two main channels and one support channel. Peers challenge choices ('Do your customers really use LinkedIn?').
4. Learners save the brief and channel map in their DIRA LEARN portfolio folder. Remind them to tell the business owner what they are doing and get agreement before posting anything for the business.

#### 1:45–1:55 · Knowledge check (10 min, assessment and feedback)
*Answer short questions on the customer journey, channels and reputation.*

1. Run the five assessment questions as a quick quiz (DIRA LEARN, a poll or on paper).
2. Go through answers and correct misunderstandings immediately.

#### 1:55–2:00 · Wrap-up and homework (5 min, conclusion and assignment)
*Summarise the session and confirm your homework.*

1. Ask three learners to say one thing they will change in their profile or business presence this week.
2. Set the homework and remind learners to bring their brief to session 4.

### 5. Step-by-step teaching content
#### 0:00–0:10 · Recap and customer answers (10 min, introduction and recap)
*Share what customers said about how they found the business.*

1. Ask four learners to share what customers told them about how they found the business. Write the channels on the board and count them.
2. Point out that word of mouth and WhatsApp usually appear often for small Kenyan businesses; this is evidence, not guesswork, and it should shape the campaign.

#### 0:10–0:30 · Target audiences and customer personas (20 min, main lesson)
*Learn how to choose a specific audience and describe them as a persona.*

1. Explain why 'everyone' is not a target audience: a small budget spread over everyone reaches no one well. A clear audience makes it easier to choose channels, words and images.
2. Teach segmentation with Shiru's Bakes. Segment 1 - working mothers in Ruiru and Juja ordering birthday cakes. Segment 2 - small offices ordering bread and snacks weekly. Segment 3 - university students at nearby campuses buying cupcakes for events. Choose one primary segment for the campaign.
3. Build a persona live: 'Mercy, 32, accountant in Juja, two children, orders a cake 4 times a year, budget KES 2,000 to 3,500, scrolls Facebook and WhatsApp status in the evening, worried about late delivery and cakes that look different from the photo, trusts recommendations from other mothers.'
4. Explain each persona part and how it changes marketing: her worry about late delivery means the campaign must show on-time delivery proof; her evening scrolling means posting at 7 to 9 pm.
5. Warn against stereotypes; personas must come from real conversations and data (the homework answers), not assumptions.

#### 0:30–0:45 · Build your customer persona (15 min, practical exercise)
*Create a persona for your campaign using the template and real customer answers.*

1. Learners fill the persona template for their brief - name, age, location, situation, goals, problems, budget, buying habits, online habits, objections, what builds trust.
2. Every persona must include at least two facts from real customer answers. Learners mark them with a star.
3. Swap with a partner, who asks two questions the persona does not yet answer. Learners update it.

#### 0:45–1:05 · Social media marketing on four platforms (20 min, main lesson)
*Compare Facebook, Instagram, TikTok and LinkedIn and how each shows content to people.*

1. Facebook: broad adult audience across Kenya, strong for local community groups, Marketplace and events. Formats - posts, photos, videos, reels, groups, Marketplace listings. Good for - local services, second-hand goods, community organisations, older customers. Tip - join and contribute to local groups (estate, town, university) and follow each group's rules on promotion.
2. Instagram: visual, younger urban audience. Formats - feed posts, carousels, reels, stories, highlights. Good for - fashion, food, beauty, photography, events. Tip - highlights for prices, how to order and reviews; a clear bio with a WhatsApp link.
3. TikTok: short video and discovery, people can find you even with zero followers. Formats - short videos, trends, sounds, duets, lives. Good for - showing products being made or used, behind the scenes, personality-led brands. Tip - the first 2 seconds decide if people keep watching.
4. LinkedIn: professional audience. Formats - posts, articles, company pages. Good for - professional services, B2B, recruitment, NGOs, a learner's own career. Tip - share lessons and results, not hard selling.
5. How feeds decide (simplified): platforms show content people are likely to engage with - watch time, saves, shares, comments and how quickly people react. Consistency and relevance beat posting a lot. Explain business or professional accounts and their insights (reach, engagement, audience age and location).
6. Quick discussion: which two platforms fit Mercy (the persona)? Facebook and WhatsApp status, maybe Instagram. Which fit a youth photographer in Kisumu? Instagram and TikTok.

#### 1:10–1:30 · Demonstration and practice - WhatsApp Business (20 min, demonstration)
*See a WhatsApp Business profile set up step by step, then plan or set up your own.*

1. Explain that WhatsApp Business is a separate free app for businesses and that it should run on a business number. Do not ask learners to switch their personal number in class.
2. Demonstrate on a test phone (or screenshots): business profile (name, category, description, address or service area, hours, email, website or Instagram), catalogue (item photo, name, price in KES, short description), greeting message ('Karibu Shiru's Bakes! Send us the cake size and date and we will reply within 30 minutes'), away message, quick replies (/prices, /delivery, /mpesa), and labels (New order, Paid, Delivered).
3. Status and broadcast lists: status is good for daily updates (today's fresh bread); broadcast lists only reach people who have saved your number, which is a good consent signal. Never add people to groups or lists without their agreement, and always offer a way to stop messages ('Reply STOP to stop updates').
4. Learners write their own WhatsApp Business plan - profile description, three catalogue items with KES prices, greeting message, away message, three quick replies and three labels. Learners who already run a business number can set it up now.

#### 0:10–0:30 · Branding and brand identity (20 min, main lesson)
*Learn what a brand is and the parts of a simple brand identity.*

1. Define brand: what people feel and expect from a business. The logo is only a symbol. Ask learners to describe the feeling of two well-known Kenyan brands (for example a telecom company and a local dairy) in three words.
2. #### 0:10–0:30 · Copywriting and persuasive messaging (20 min, main lesson)
*Learn how to write hooks, benefits, calls to action and honest persuasive captions.*

1. Hooks: the first line must stop the scroll. Types - a question ('Late cake again?'), a bold promise ('Birthday cakes delivered on time, every time'), a number ('3 ways to save on your child's party'), a story start ('At 6 am on Saturday...').
2. Features versus benefits. Feature - 'eggless sponge'. Benefit - 'everyone at the party can enjoy it, even guests who don't eat eggs'. Practise turning two features into benefits together.
3. AIDA: Attention (hook), Interest (relevant detail), Desire (benefit, proof), Action (call to action). Write one AIDA caption live for Shiru's Bakes.
4. PAS: Problem ('Your office tea break needs snacks but the shop is far'), Agitate ('so people leave work and come back late'), Solution ('Shiru's delivers fresh bread and mandazi to your office every morning - order by 8 pm').
5. Calls to action must be one clear step: 'WhatsApp us on 07XX', 'Tap the link to order', 'Save this post for your next party'.
6. Tone: match the persona. Many Kenyan brands mix English with Kiswahili or Sheng, but it must sound natural to the audience. Honesty: do not claim 'best in Kenya' without proof, do not invent reviews, do not hide prices or conditions, and label paid partnerships.

#### 0:30–0:45 · Write your campaign captions (15 min, practical exercise)
*Write three captions for your calendar posts using AIDA or PAS.*

1. Learners write three captions for posts from their calendar - one AIDA, one PAS and one short caption for a status or story.
2. Each caption must include a hook, a benefit, proof or detail, and one call to action.
3. Partners swap captions and underline the hook and CTA. If they cannot find them, the writer rewrites.

#### 0:45–1:00 · Customer engagement and conversion (15 min, main lesson)
*Learn how to turn likes, comments and messages into orders and repeat customers.*

1. Explain conversion: the moment someone takes the action you want (a message, an order, a booking, a sign-up). Engagement without conversion does not pay the rent.
2. Speed: reply to enquiries quickly during business hours; many customers buy from whoever answers first. Use quick replies from session 4.
3. The enquiry-to-order flow: greet, answer the question with the price, ask one question to understand the need, recommend, confirm details (size, date, location), give payment details (M-Pesa till or paybill, never a personal number for a registered business), confirm the order in writing, thank and follow up after delivery.
4. Social proof: share reviews and customer photos with their consent, numbers ('120 cakes delivered this month'), before-and-after.
5. Offers and urgency: a genuine limited offer can help ('10% off orders before Friday'), but fake countdowns and pressure damage trust.
6. Reduce friction: prices in posts or a catalogue (customers who have to ask 'how much?' often leave), delivery areas and costs, clear payment steps.
7. Retention: a thank-you message, asking for a review, and a reason to come back (a loyalty stamp on WhatsApp, a birthday message with permission).

#### 1:15–1:30 · Search engine optimisation fundamentals and Google Business Profile (15 min, main lesson)
*Learn how search works and how a small business gets found on Google Search and Maps.*

1. Explain search in three steps: search engines crawl the web, index (store) pages, then rank results by relevance to the search, quality and trust, and for local searches, distance and prominence.
2. Keywords and intent: the words people actually type. 'cake shop Ruiru', 'birthday cake delivery Juja', 'eggless cake near me'. Informational intent (how to) versus commercial intent (where to buy). Use Google autocomplete and 'People also ask' to find real phrases.
3. Local SEO is the most useful part for small Kenyan businesses: a complete Google Business Profile shows on Google Maps and local results. Demonstrate the parts - business name (real name, no keyword stuffing), category, service area or address, hours, phone, website or social link, photos, products or services, posts, and reviews and replies.
4. Reviews: ask happy customers for Google reviews with a short link; never buy fake reviews or offer payment for positive ones.
5. On-page basics if the business has a website or blog: a clear page title and description with the main keyword, a heading that says what and where, fast pages, mobile-friendly, contact details on every page.
6. Consistency: the same business name, phone and location on Google, Facebook, Instagram and directories helps search engines and customers trust the business.

### 5. Step-by-step teaching content
#### 0:00–0:10 · Recap and results from your first post (10 min, introduction and recap)
*Share the reach and engagement from the post you published.*

1. Learners share the reach and engagement of the post they published. Note the range and ask what might explain the differences (time, hook, image, audience).
2. Say that today is about measuring and improving, not just posting.

#### 0:10–0:30 · Introduction to online advertising (20 min, main lesson)
*Learn how paid ads work on social media and search, and when a small business should use them.*

1. Organic versus paid: organic posts reach a part of your followers; paid ads reach people chosen by targeting. Paid works best when the offer, content and WhatsApp follow-up are already good - ads amplify, they do not fix a weak offer.
2. Boost versus Ads Manager: boosting a post is quick (choose audience, budget, duration); Meta Ads Manager gives more control (objective, placements, multiple ads, testing). Show the boost screen and the Ads Manager campaign structure (campaign - objective; ad set - audience, budget, schedule; ad - creative and copy) using screenshots.
3. Objectives: choose the one that matches the campaign goal - for a WhatsApp business, a 'messages' or 'engagement with WhatsApp' style objective is often better than reach. Names change on the platforms, so check the current options in Ads Manager.
4. Targeting: location radius (for example 10 km around Ruiru), age, interests, and custom audiences (people who engaged with your page). Too narrow costs more; too broad wastes money.
5. Budget: platforms allow small daily budgets; check the current minimum in Ads Manager. Start small (for example a few hundred shillings a day for a few days), test two versions, keep the better one.
6. Google Search ads appear for searches with commercial intent ('cake delivery Juja'); you pay per click. Explain briefly; most learners will start with Meta.
7. Policies and ethics: ads are reviewed and can be rejected; no misleading claims, before-and-after body images, or restricted products. Paid influencer posts must be disclosed (for example 'ad' or 'paid partnership').
8. Payment: ad accounts are paid by card or other methods the platform accepts; the business owner must authorise any spending. Learners must never spend the owner's money without written agreement.

#### 0:30–0:45 · Campaign planning and budgeting in KES (15 min, main lesson)
*Plan a campaign timeline and a realistic budget with an estimated cost per result.*

1. SMART campaign objective: 'Get 20 new cake orders through WhatsApp from Ruiru and Juja between 1 and 14 December' rather than 'increase sales'.
2. Show the budget sheet (Google Sheets or paper): lines for content (printing, props, sample products for photos), internet data, ads, delivery promotion, incentives (discount cost), and contingency. Use the Shiru's Bakes example totalling about KES 3,000, including KES 1,500 for ads.
3. Cost per result: if KES 1,500 of ads brings 30 WhatsApp conversations and 10 orders, cost per conversation = KES 50, cost per order = KES 150. Compare with the profit per order (for example KES 600). If the profit is higher than the cost per order, the ads pay for themselves.
4. Zero-budget campaigns are valid - use organic posts, WhatsApp status, broadcast lists, community groups and referral offers. Learners with no ad budget plan time instead of money.

#### 0:45–1:00 · Marketing analytics and measuring results (15 min, main lesson)
*Choose KPIs for your objective and learn how to read and track the numbers.*

1. Teach the measures: reach (unique people who saw it), impressions (times shown), engagement rate (likes, comments, shares, saves divided by reach), clicks, messages started, conversions (orders, bookings), cost per result, return on ad spend (revenue from ads divided by ad cost).
2. Link KPIs to objectives: an awareness campaign tracks reach and video views; a sales campaign tracks messages, orders and cost per order. Vanity metrics (likes alone) can mislead.
3. Show where to find numbers: Facebook and Instagram professional dashboards and insights, Meta Business Suite, WhatsApp Business (message statistics where available), TikTok analytics for business or creator accounts.
4. Track offline and WhatsApp sales: ask every customer 'how did you hear about us?', use a campaign code ('mention CAKE10 for 10% off'), and record orders in a simple tracking sheet (date, customer first name or code, source, amount, campaign code).
5. Show the tracking sheet and how to calculate weekly totals. Explain that learning from results ('reels brought more messages than photos, so do more reels') is the real point.

#### 1:00–1:05 · Stretch break (5 min, stretch break)
*A five-minute stretch and movement break (seated option available).*

1. Invite everyone to stand, stretch, walk or drink water for five minutes; offer a seated option for anyone who prefers or needs it.
2. Restart on time with a one-line reminder of where the session is and what comes next.

#### 1:05–1:35 · Campaign build (30 min, practical exercise)
*Assemble your complete campaign plan using the campaign template.*

1. Learners open the campaign template and fill every section: 1 brief; 2 SMART objective; 3 persona; 4 channels and platform plan; 5 brand mini-kit; 6 key message and offer; 7 two-week content calendar; 8 three finished posts with captions and one WhatsApp broadcast or status message; 9 optional ad plan (objective, audience, creative, budget, duration); 10 budget in KES; 11 KPIs, targets and tracking method; 12 timeline and who does what.
2. The facilitator circulates and checks each campaign for consistency - does the persona match the platforms, the message match the brand, the KPI match the objective?
3. Learners who finish early create one more asset or a short reel for their campaign.

#### 1:35–1:50 · Peer testing and revision (15 min, group activity)
*Test your campaign with peers using the checklist and improve it.*

1. In groups of three, each learner presents the campaign in three minutes. Peers use the campaign checklist (clear objective, specific persona, fitting channels, consistent brand, strong hooks, clear CTA, realistic budget, measurable KPIs, consent and honesty) and give two strengths and two improvements.
2. Learners make at least one improvement immediately and note the others to finish before session 8.

#### 1:50–2:00 · Showcase preparation and homework (10 min, conclusion and assignment)
*Learn how session 8 works and what to prepare.*

1. Explain the showcase format: three minutes to present (introduce yourself with your self-pitch, the brief, the campaign, any results so far and what you would do next), two minutes of questions. Share the showcase rubric.
2. Homework: finish the campaign, run at least one part of it in real life (publish posts or a status campaign with permission) and record the results, prepare a five-slide presentation or a one-page visual.

## Session 8 (Week 4, 120 minutes): Digital marketing campaign showcase

*Present your digital marketing campaign, answer questions, give and receive feedback, pitch yourself professionally and plan your next 30 days.*

### 1. Learning purpose
Learners demonstrate their practical digital marketing capability by presenting the campaign they built, explaining their choices and results, and answering questions. They learn to present their work as a portfolio piece to employers and clients, practise professional behaviour in a showcase setting and leave with a 30-day action plan.

### 2. Learning objectives
By the end of the session learners can:
- Present a basic digital marketing campaign clearly in three minutes, explaining objective, audience, channels, content, budget and measures
- Report real results or test findings honestly and say what they would improve
- Answer questions about their choices with evidence
- Give and receive feedback professionally using the showcase rubric
- Pitch themselves and their campaign as a portfolio piece to an employer or client
- Write a 30-day action plan for continuing to market the business or to find marketing work

### 5. Step-by-step teaching content
#### 0:00–0:10 · Showcase set-up and expectations (10 min, introduction and recap)
*Learn the showcase order, rubric and etiquette.*

2. Explain the format: three minutes to present, two minutes of questions, then the next presenter. Share the rubric: clarity of objective and audience (20%), quality and consistency of content (25%), channel and budget choices (20%), measurement and results (20%), presentation and answers (15%).
3. Etiquette: phones on silent, cameras on if online, respectful questions, applause after each presenter.
4. Assign each learner to give written feedback on two specific presenters (so every presenter gets at least two pieces of peer feedback).

#### 0:10–0:40 · Campaign presentations and questions - block 1 (30 min, assessment and feedback)
*The first six presenters in each room present and answer questions; give rubric feedback to your two assigned peers.*

1. Run the campaigns with the session 8 room plan in the facilitator instructions: the first six presenters in each room go in this block, each with 3 minutes plus 2 minutes of questions.
2. Learners present in the published order. Keep strict time with a visible timer; give a 30-second warning.
3. Each presentation covers: self-pitch introduction; the brief; SMART objective; persona; channels; brand and three posts; budget; KPIs and results or test findings; what they learned and would do next.
4. Ask at least one question per presenter, such as: 'Why did you choose this platform for this persona?', 'What would you change if you had KES 5,000 more?', 'What does your cost per result tell you?', 'How did you get consent for these photos?'

#### 0:45–1:15 · Campaign presentations and questions - block 2 (30 min, assessment and feedback)
*The next six presenters in each room present; recorded presenters answer their live questions.*

1. The remaining presenters in each live room (7 to 12) go now, with the same timer, rubric and question approach as block 1.
2. In a recorded-questions room, do not replay the recordings (the panel scored them before the session); give each recorded presenter 2 minutes of live questions about their campaign.
3. Each room's panel hands its completed campaign rubric sheets to the room facilitator before the feedback round.

#### 1:15–1:25 · Feedback round (10 min, group activity)

2. Facilitator summarises patterns: what strong campaigns had in common (specific persona, consistent brand, clear CTA, honest measurement) and the most common improvements.

#### 1:25–1:40 · Pitching yourself and workplace readiness (15 min, main lesson)
*Turn your campaign into a portfolio piece and practise pitching yourself to an employer or client.*

1. Show how to turn the campaign into a one-page case study: the challenge, what you did, results, what you learned, and visuals. Add it to LinkedIn (Featured section), a Google Drive portfolio folder or a simple online portfolio.
2. Pitch formula for interviews and clients: who you are, what you can do, proof (this campaign and its numbers), what you are looking for. Example: 'I am Brian, a digital marketer from Thika. I recently ran a two-week WhatsApp and Facebook campaign for a bakery that brought 14 new orders for KES 1,200 in ads. I am looking for an internship or small businesses that need help with social media.'
3. Pathways: internships and entry-level jobs (marketing assistant, social media assistant, customer service), freelance and online work (Kenya's Ajira Digital programme provides training and information on online work), and supporting or running a business. Warn again about job scams that ask for fees.
4. Professional follow-up: within two days, thank the business owner, share the results summary and ask for a short testimonial or reference; connect with guests on LinkedIn with a personal note.
