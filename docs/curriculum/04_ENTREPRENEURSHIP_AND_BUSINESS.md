# DIRA LEARN V13.2 curriculum – Entrepreneurship & Business

Revised 19 Nov 2026. Status: **reviewed draft lesson plans (V13.2), authorised for approval by programme management.** Durations are the approved timetable and were not changed.

**Track focus.** Turn an idea into a tested business concept. Learners spot opportunities, understand customer problems and markets, design a value proposition and business model, learn practical money skills (costing, pricing, revenue, expenses and cash flow), market and sell ethically with good customer service, test their idea with real customers, and pitch a business concept.

**Final project.** A business concept and pitch for an early-stage idea. It includes an opportunity statement backed by customer interviews, a value proposition, a one-page business model, a start-up budget and one-month cash-flow estimate with pricing, a one-page marketing and customer-experience plan, evidence from at least one real test, a 3-minute pitch in session 8 and a 30-day validation action. Privacy option: the business can be a simulated idea, and customer evidence is shown in the pitch without names, photos or contact details unless the person agreed.

## Session 3 (Week 2, 120 minutes): Opportunity spotting

*Identify customer needs and business opportunities; distinguish assumptions from evidence.*

### 1. Learning purpose
Learners learn to find business opportunities in real problems around them, understand customers' problems deeply, do simple market research, and describe customer needs and target markets. They separate assumptions from evidence, prepare an interview guide and write a one-page opportunity statement.

### 2. Learning objectives
By the end of the session learners can:
- Identify business opportunities from problems, trends, skills and resources in their community
- Describe a customer problem in terms of the job the customer is trying to do, their pains and the gains they want
- Distinguish assumptions from evidence and list the riskiest assumptions in an idea
- Plan simple market research using primary (observation, interviews, surveys) and secondary (reports, data) sources
- Describe a target market and early adopters for an idea
- Conduct a practice customer interview using good, non-leading questions
- Write a one-page opportunity statement

    - Willingness to pay

    - Secondary research - KNBS data, county information, industry reports, news
    - Asking good questions (past behaviour, not opinions about the future)
    - Avoiding leading questions
- **Customer needs and target markets**

### 5. Step-by-step teaching content
#### 0:00–0:10 · Welcome to Entrepreneurship & Business and track overview (10 min, introduction and recap)
*Meet your track group and see the six sessions and final business pitch.*

1. Show the journey: S3 opportunity spotting, S4 value proposition and business model, S5 money - costing, pricing and cash flow, S6 marketing, customer experience and sales, S7 test and pitch, S8 pitch showcase.
2. Explain the final project - a tested business concept and a 3-minute pitch. Say that the goal is evidence, not a perfect plan.
3. Ask learners to share in one sentence a business they admire in their area and why.

#### 0:10–0:30 · Identifying business opportunities (20 min, main lesson)
*Learn where business opportunities come from and how to spot them.*

1. Define an opportunity: a problem or need that enough people have, that they would pay to solve, and that you can realistically solve. An idea is a possible solution; an opportunity is the problem plus a market.
2. Sources of opportunities: (1) problems people complain about (water shortages, unreliable deliveries, expensive school uniforms); (2) trends (mobile money, online shopping, solar, recycling, urban farming, a growing youth population); (3) your skills and networks (you can cook, fix phones, know farmers); (4) gaps (no laundry service near a university hostel).
3. Kenyan examples: a young person in Nakuru who saw students struggling to dry clothes in the rainy season and started a laundry pick-up service; a group in Machakos turning mango waste into dried mango snacks; a Kibera youth group collecting plastic for recycling companies.
4. Opportunity filter - ask four questions: Is the problem real and frequent? Do people pay to solve it today? Can I reach these customers? Can I start small with what I have?
5. Learners list three problems they have seen this week in their community.

#### 0:30–0:50 · Understanding customer problems (20 min, main lesson)
*Look at problems from the customer's point of view - the job, the pains and the gains.*

1. The job: what the customer is trying to get done. A mother buying a school uniform is not buying cloth; she is getting her child ready for school on time, looking smart, within budget.
2. Pains: what annoys, costs or worries them (long queues, poor quality, prices going up, unreliable sellers). Gains: what would delight them (durability, delivery, paying in instalments with M-Pesa).
3. How they solve it today: the current alternative is your real competition - even if it is 'doing nothing' or 'asking a cousin'.
4. Frequency, intensity and willingness to pay: a problem that happens daily and hurts a lot is a better opportunity than one that happens once a year and is mildly annoying.
5. Assumptions versus evidence: 'students will pay KES 100 for laundry pick-up' is an assumption until real students say they have paid for similar services or actually pay. List assumptions and mark the riskiest - the one that would kill the idea if wrong.
6. Work through one learner's problem as a group, mapping job, pains, gains and assumptions on the board.

#### 0:50–1:05 · Market research and target markets (15 min, main lesson)
*Learn simple ways to research a market and define your target customers.*

1. Primary research: observe (count customers at a competitor's stall at different times), interview (ten conversations teach more than a hundred opinions online), short surveys (a Google Form shared in a relevant WhatsApp group, with the admin's permission).
2. Secondary research: the Kenya National Bureau of Statistics (KNBS) publishes population and economic data; county governments publish plans and statistics; industry and news reports give trends. Always note the source and date.
3. Target market and early adopters: the target market is the specific group you serve first; early adopters are the people with the problem most urgently who are already trying to solve it - they will try something new first.
4. Rough market size: how many potential customers in your area, how often they buy, and how much they spend. Example - 2,000 students in nearby hostels, 30% send laundry out, twice a month, KES 150 each.
5. Competition and alternatives: who else solves the problem and how; what you could do differently.

#### 1:05–1:10 · Stretch break (5 min, stretch break)
*A five-minute stretch and movement break (seated option available).*

1. Invite everyone to stand, stretch, walk or drink water for five minutes; offer a seated option for anyone who prefers or needs it.
2. Restart on time with a one-line reminder of where the session is and what comes next.

#### 1:10–1:25 · Demonstration - good and bad customer interviews (15 min, demonstration)
*See the difference between a leading interview and a good one that uncovers real behaviour.*

1. Role-play a bad interview with a co-facilitator or volunteer: 'Would you buy my amazing juice? It is healthy and cheap, don't you think?' Point out leading questions, talking about the idea, and asking about the future.
2. Role-play a good interview: 'Tell me about the last time you bought juice or a snack in the afternoon. Where? How much? What did you like or dislike? What else did you consider?' Point out questions about past behaviour, listening more than talking, and not pitching.
3. Share the interview guide template: warm-up, the problem (last time it happened), current solution, pains, spending, what an ideal solution would do, permission to follow up. Ten questions maximum.

#### 1:25–1:40 · Interview guide and practice interviews (15 min, group activity)
*Write your interview guide and practise interviewing in pairs.*

1. Learners choose the opportunity they will develop for the track (from their three problems or an existing idea) and write an interview guide of eight to ten questions.
2. In pairs, learners interview each other (5 minutes each) as if the partner were a potential customer. The interviewer notes what they learned; the partner gives feedback on any leading questions.
3. Learners revise their guide based on the feedback.

#### 1:40–1:55 · Opportunity statement (15 min, practical exercise)
*Write a one-page opportunity statement for your idea.*

1. Learners complete the one-page opportunity statement: the problem, who has it (target market and early adopters), how they solve it today, why that is not good enough, the opportunity, rough market size, top three assumptions (riskiest first), and how they will gather evidence this week.
2. The facilitator checks a few statements and gives quick feedback on specificity.

#### 1:55–2:00 · Wrap-up and homework (5 min, conclusion and assignment)
*Summarise and confirm the homework.*

1. Ask two learners to read their opportunity sentence.
2. Set the homework - at least five real interviews before session 4.

### 5. Step-by-step teaching content
#### 0:00–0:10 · Recap and interview findings (10 min, introduction and recap)
*Share what your customer interviews revealed.*

1. Ask four learners to share one surprise from their interviews (a quote, a number or a behaviour).
2. Highlight learners whose interviews changed their idea - that is a success, not a failure.

#### 0:10–0:30 · Value propositions (20 min, main lesson)
*Learn how to match your offer to customers' jobs, pains and gains.*

1. Draw the value proposition canvas. Customer profile (circle) - jobs, pains, gains from interviews. Value map (square) - products and services, pain relievers (how you reduce pains), gain creators (how you create gains). Fit = your pain relievers and gain creators address the most important pains and gains.
2. Worked example - laundry pick-up for students. Pains - no space to dry clothes, rain, time lost walking to the laundry. Pain relievers - pick-up from the hostel, dried and folded within 24 hours. Gains - more free time, clean clothes for events. Gain creators - weekly subscription, M-Pesa payment, WhatsApp reminders.
3. Value proposition statement formula: 'For [target customer] who [need or problem], [business name] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [alternative], we [key difference].' Example - 'For students in Nakuru hostels who have no time or space to wash and dry clothes, Fresh Fold is a laundry pick-up service that returns clothes clean and folded within 24 hours. Unlike hand-washing or walking to town, we collect from your hostel and you pay by M-Pesa.'
4. Differentiation: why customers would choose you over the current alternative - faster, cheaper, closer, more reliable, more convenient, better quality.

#### 0:30–0:45 · Your value proposition (15 min, practical exercise)
*Complete the value proposition canvas and statement for your idea using interview evidence.*

1. Learners fill the customer profile with evidence from their interviews (mark each item with the number of interviewees who mentioned it).
2. They fill the value map and check that each top pain and gain has a pain reliever or gain creator.
3. They write the one-sentence value proposition statement and read it to a partner, who says whether it is clear and believable.

#### 0:45–1:05 · Business models and the simplified canvas (20 min, main lesson)
*Learn the parts of a business model and common model types in Kenya.*

1. Explain the simplified business model canvas - nine boxes: customers (who); value proposition (what); channels (how they find, buy and receive); customer relationships (how you keep them); revenue streams (how money comes in); key activities (what you must do); key resources (what you need - people, equipment, money, skills); key partners (who helps - suppliers, riders, landlords); costs (what you spend).
2. Fill the canvas for Fresh Fold as a worked example, box by box.
3. Business model types with Kenyan examples: product sales (a juice vendor); services (a mobile phone repair shop); subscriptions (weekly laundry, gym membership); commissions and marketplaces (platforms that connect sellers and buyers and take a fee); rental (renting out tents and chairs for events); freemium (free basic service, paid premium).
4. Ask learners which model type fits their idea and whether a different one could work better.

#### 1:10–1:20 · Reaching and keeping customers - marketing in the business model (10 min, main lesson)
*See where the approved digital marketing topics fit in the channels and relationships boxes of your model.*

1. Explain that channels and customer relationships are where marketing lives in a business model. For most young Kenyan businesses, the main channels are digital: social media (Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn), WhatsApp Business (catalogue, quick replies, status), content (photos, graphics in tools like Canva, captions with calls to action), and sometimes advertising and search (a Google Business Profile so people find you on Maps).
2. At the business-model level, decide three things: which two channels will bring your first customers, what one message will make them act, and how you will measure whether the channel works (enquiries, orders, cost per customer).
3. Explain that session 6 turns this into a one-page marketing plan, and that the Digital Marketing track teaches these channels in depth. Here the question is only whether the channels fit the customers and the costs in the model.

#### 1:20–1:40 · Map your one-page business model (20 min, practical exercise)
*Complete the simplified business model canvas for your idea.*

1. Learners complete the simplified canvas for their idea on one page, using their value proposition and interview evidence.
2. In the channels box, they choose two first channels and how they will measure them.
3. They mark each box as 'evidence' (from interviews or data) or 'assumption'.
4. The facilitator circulates and asks 'how do you know?' about key boxes (revenue and channels especially).

#### 1:40–1:55 · Peer challenge (15 min, group activity)

In groups of three, one learner presents their model in 2 minutes; the other two act as challengers with question cards: 'Why would customers switch?', 'How will the first 10 customers find you?', 'What does one customer cost you?', 'What happens if your key partner lets you down?', 'Which box is your biggest assumption?' ## Session 5 (Week 3, 120 minutes): Basic venture finance

*Identify start-up and operating costs, price, revenue and simple cash flow.*

### 1. Learning purpose
Learners build basic financial literacy for running a small business - separating business and personal money, keeping records, understanding credit - and learn to cost a product, set a price, calculate profit and break-even, and plan cash flow. They produce a start-up budget, a one-month cash-flow estimate and a lean one-page business plan outline.

### 2. Learning objectives
By the end of the session learners can:
- Explain why business and personal money must be separated and how to keep simple records (including M-Pesa statements)
- Describe the risks and costs of borrowing (digital loans, Hustler Fund, chamas, SACCOs, banks)
- Classify start-up and operating costs, and fixed and variable costs
- Calculate variable cost per unit, contribution per unit, gross profit and fully allocated unit cost, and set a price using cost-plus, competitor and value methods
- Calculate break-even in units using contribution per unit
- Explain the difference between profit and cash flow, and build a one-month cash-flow estimate
- Outline a lean one-page business plan

### 3. Topics and subtopics
- **Basic financial literacy for entrepreneurs**
    - Separating business and personal money

    - Savings and emergency funds

    - Tax basics - KRA PIN and why records matter
- **Pricing and costing**
    - Start-up versus operating costs
    - Fixed versus variable costs
    - Variable cost per unit and fully allocated unit cost
    - Cost-plus pricing
    - Competitor-based pricing
    - Value-based pricing
    - Discounts and their effect on profit
- **Revenue and expenses**
    - Revenue (sales)
    - Cost of goods sold
    - Contribution per unit and contribution margin
    - Gross profit and net profit
    - Simple monthly profit statement
    - Break-even
- **Cash-flow fundamentals**
    - Profit is not cash
    - Timing of money in and out
    - Credit sales and stock
    - One-month cash-flow estimate
    - Keeping a cash buffer
- **Business planning**
    - Lean one-page business plan
    - What to include
    - When a full business plan is needed (loans, investors, competitions)

### 5. Step-by-step teaching content
#### 0:00–0:10 · Recap and cost hunt results (10 min, introduction and recap)
*Share the real costs you found.*

1. Ask learners to share one cost they found that surprised them (rent, permits, packaging, transport).
2. Explain that many small businesses in Kenya fail not because nobody buys, but because they do not know their numbers. Today is about numbers.

#### 0:10–0:25 · Basic financial literacy for entrepreneurs (15 min, main lesson)
*Learn the money habits that keep a small business alive.*

1. Separate business and personal money: use a separate M-Pesa line, a till number or a separate account for the business. Mixing them makes it impossible to know if the business makes money.
2. Records: write every sale and expense daily in a book or simple sheet; download M-Pesa statements monthly. Records help you make decisions, apply for loans and handle tax.
3. Pay yourself a fixed amount instead of taking money from the till whenever you need it.
4. Savings: set aside a little each week for emergencies and stock.
5. Credit: digital loans are quick but can be expensive and can lead to a cycle of borrowing; late repayment can affect your credit record with credit reference bureaus. Government funds such as the Hustler Fund, chamas and SACCOs are alternatives with different terms. Before borrowing, ask - what exactly will this money buy, how will it bring more income, and what is the total amount I will repay?
6. Tax basics: registering for a KRA PIN is required for many formal activities; keeping records makes tax obligations easier to meet. Check current requirements on the official KRA website.

#### 0:25–0:45 · Costing (20 min, main lesson)
*Classify your costs and calculate the cost of one unit.*

1. Start-up costs (once, before opening): equipment, first stock, registration and permits, initial branding. Operating costs (every month): rent, stock, transport, data, wages, packaging.
2. Fixed costs (same every month regardless of sales): rent, a stall fee, a salary. Variable costs (change with each unit sold): ingredients, packaging, delivery per order.
3. Variable cost per unit - a juice business. Per 500 ml bottle: fruit KES 25, sugar and water KES 5, bottle and label KES 15, fruit transport share KES 5. Variable cost per bottle = KES 50 (these are also the direct costs of making the product). Fixed costs per month: stall fee KES 3,000, blender depreciation KES 500, data KES 500 = KES 4,000.
4. Fully allocated unit cost = variable cost per unit + (fixed costs for the month / units sold in the month). At 300 bottles a month: 50 + 4,000 / 300 = 50 + 13.33 = about KES 63 per bottle. At only 150 bottles it rises to 50 + 26.67 = about KES 77: fixed costs spread over fewer units, so each costs more.
5. Learners classify their own cost list into start-up or operating and fixed or variable.

#### 0:45–1:00 · Pricing and break-even (15 min, main lesson)
*Set a price using three methods, then calculate contribution per unit and break-even.*

1. Cost-plus pricing: add a markup to cost. A 60% markup on the variable cost gives KES 50 + 30 = KES 80. Check it against the fully allocated unit cost at realistic sales (KES 63 at 300 bottles): KES 80 leaves about KES 17 per bottle after all costs.
2. Competitor-based pricing: what do similar juice sellers charge nearby? If they charge KES 70 to 100, KES 80 fits.
3. Value-based pricing: what is it worth to the customer? Fresh juice delivered to offices may be worth KES 100.
4. Two floors for any price: never below the variable cost per unit (every sale would lose money), and over the month the price must be above the fully allocated unit cost at your realistic sales volume, or the business makes a loss. Then check it fits the market and reflects the value.
5. Contribution per unit = price - variable cost per unit = KES 80 - 50 = KES 30. It is what each sale contributes towards fixed costs and then profit. Contribution margin = 30 / 80 = 37.5% of the price (a 60% markup on cost is a 37.5% margin on price - markup and margin are not the same).
6. Break-even (units) = fixed costs / contribution per unit = 4,000 / 30 = 133.3, so 134 bottles a month (round up) - about 5.2 a day over 26 trading days, so plan for at least 6. Check: at break-even the fully allocated unit cost (50 + 4,000 / 134 = about KES 80) equals the price.
7. Discounts: a 10% discount makes the price KES 72, so contribution falls from KES 30 to KES 22 per bottle - a 27% drop - and break-even rises from 134 to 182 bottles (4,000 / 22 = 181.8). Use discounts carefully.

#### 1:05–1:25 · Revenue, expenses and cash flow (20 min, main lesson)
*Understand profit and cash flow and why they differ.*

1. Simple monthly profit at 300 bottles: revenue 300 x KES 80 = KES 24,000; cost of goods sold (the direct costs of the bottles sold) 300 x KES 50 = KES 15,000; gross profit = revenue - cost of goods sold = KES 9,000; minus fixed costs KES 4,000 = net profit KES 5,000 (before tax). Here every variable cost is a direct product cost, so gross profit equals total contribution (300 x KES 30). If a business also has variable selling costs, such as a delivery rider paid per order or an agent's commission, those reduce contribution but not gross profit, and the two figures differ. Check: fully allocated cost 300 x KES 63.33 = KES 19,000, and 24,000 - 19,000 = KES 5,000.
2. Formula card for the finance template: variable cost per unit = sum of the costs of one unit; contribution per unit = price - variable cost per unit; contribution margin = contribution per unit / price; gross profit = revenue - cost of goods sold; net profit = gross profit - fixed (and other operating) costs; fully allocated unit cost = variable cost per unit + fixed costs / units; break-even units = fixed costs / contribution per unit, rounded up.
3. Cash flow is the timing of money in and out. A business can be profitable on paper and still run out of cash - for example if an office pays at month-end but you must buy fruit every morning.
4. Show a one-month cash-flow table by week: opening cash, cash in (sales paid), cash out (stock, rent, transport), closing cash. Show a week where closing cash goes negative and discuss solutions (smaller stock purchases, deposits from customers, shorter credit, a cash buffer).
5. Credit sales and stock both tie up cash; record who owes you and follow up politely.

#### 0:10–0:30 · Testing business ideas (20 min, main lesson)

- Business model, finance template and interview notes from earlier sessions

### 12. Assessment questions and activities
| Question or task | What a good answer shows |
|---|---|
| What is a minimum viable product? | The simplest version of an offer that lets you learn whether customers want it. |
| Why set a success criterion before testing? | To avoid interpreting results to fit what you hoped. |
| Name three parts of a business pitch. | Any three of hook, problem, solution, evidence, model and numbers, team, ask. |
| What should you do with pre-order money if you decide to stop? | Refund it. |

### 13. Expected learner outputs
- Test card (and results if already tested)
- Completed lean one-page business plan
- Three-minute pitch with up to five slides or a one-page visual
- Peer review notes and revised assumptions

### 14. Facilitator instructions
**Before the session**
- Prepare the test card, lean plan and pitch templates, the pitch rubric and the example pitch.
- Check current official information on business registration and permits if you plan to mention specifics.
- Set the session 8 room plan from the track group size (up to 12 - one room; 13 to 24 - two rooms; 25 to 36 - three rooms, or two live rooms plus a recorded-questions room) and line up co-facilitators and peer panels.

**During the session**
- Keep pushing for evidence; a pitch without evidence is a wish.
- Learners whose test showed the idea does not work should be praised and helped to pitch what they learned and their changed idea.

**Inclusion and safeguarding**
- Tests must be ethical - no taking money for things learners cannot deliver; refunds if they stop.
- Learners must not borrow money to run a test.
- Consent and privacy: before using an identifiable person (face, voice or name), a photograph of someone, or a real business's name, logo, prices, customer details or results, the learner gets permission from that person or owner - in writing (a WhatsApp message is enough) for anything shown outside the class. Never children. Learners who cannot or do not wish to publish publicly use the private or simulated option, which is assessed with the same rubric and never marked down.

**After the session**
- Check every learner has a pitch draft and a test plan; message those at risk.
- Record attendance.

### 15. Homework and follow-up
- Run your test this week and record the result and your decision (continue, change or stop). Keep tests honest: do not take money for something you cannot deliver, and do not share customers' details.
- Add the result to your pitch and practise it three times within 3 minutes.
- Upload your business concept (opportunity statement, business model, finance template, marketing plan, lean plan) to DIRA LEARN.
- If you are presenting by recording (a large group, or you cannot present live), record your 3-minute pitch presentation - video, or a voice note with slides for low data - and upload it to DIRA LEARN by the day before session 8.

### 16. How this differs from other tracks
- Every track builds its final project in session 7. The Entrepreneurship & Business project is judged on evidence, sound numbers and a clear pitch, not on how big the idea is.

## Session 8 (Week 4, 120 minutes): Business pitch showcase

*Present an early-stage business concept and identify a realistic validation step.*

### 1. Learning purpose
Learners pitch their early-stage business concept with evidence, answer questions about their numbers and assumptions, give and receive feedback, learn to present themselves as entrepreneurs and job candidates, and leave with a 30-day validation action plan.

### 2. Learning objectives
By the end of the session learners can:
- Deliver a 3-minute business pitch with evidence and a specific ask
- Answer questions about customers, numbers and assumptions
- Give and receive feedback professionally using the rubric
- Pitch themselves as entrepreneurs and as employable young people using the project as proof
- Write a 30-day validation action plan

### 3. Topics and subtopics
- **Pitching and presenting business ideas**
    - Delivering the pitch
    - Answering questions
    - Handling tough questions calmly
- **Pitching yourself**
    - Entrepreneur self-pitch
    - Using the project in job applications - skills employers value
- **Professional behaviour and workplace readiness**
    - Showcase etiquette
    - Receiving critical feedback
    - Following up with guests and potential partners
    - Pathways - starting, joining a business, employment, further learning
- **Next steps**
    - 30-day validation plan
    - Support and networks for young entrepreneurs

### 4. Time allocation
| Time | Minutes | Segment | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:10 | 10 | Showcase set-up and expectations | Introduction and recap |
| 0:10–0:40 | 30 | Pitches and questions - block 1 | Assessment and feedback |
| 0:40–0:45 | 5 | Stretch break | Stretch break |
| 0:45–1:15 | 30 | Pitches and questions - block 2 | Assessment and feedback |
| 1:15–1:25 | 10 | Feedback round | Group activity |
| 1:25–1:40 | 15 | Pitching yourself and workplace readiness | Main lesson |
| 1:40–1:55 | 15 | 30-day validation action plan | Practical exercise |
| 1:55–2:00 | 5 | Celebration and close | Conclusion and assignment |
| **Total** | **120** | Equals the timetable (120 min) | |

### 5. Step-by-step teaching content
#### 0:00–0:10 · Showcase set-up and expectations (10 min, introduction and recap)
*Learn the showcase order, rubric and etiquette.*

1. Welcome learners and guests (local entrepreneurs, DIRA staff, mentors, potential partners). Thank interviewees and test customers.
2. Share the rubric: problem and customer (20%), solution and value proposition (15%), evidence from testing (25%), business model and numbers (20%), pitch delivery and answers (20%).
3. Assign each learner two presenters to give written feedback to.

#### 0:10–0:40 · Pitches and questions - block 1 (30 min, assessment and feedback)
*The first six presenters in each room pitch and answer questions; give rubric feedback to your two assigned peers.*

1. Run the pitches with the session 8 room plan in the facilitator instructions: the first six pitchers in each room go in this block, each with 3 minutes plus 2 minutes of questions.
2. Learners pitch in order, 3 minutes each plus 2 minutes of questions. Strict timer with a 30-second warning.
3. Ask at least one question per presenter, such as: 'How many customers have actually paid?', 'What is your break-even and how long will it take to reach?', 'What did your test tell you?', 'What would you do with KES 10,000?', 'What is your biggest risk?'
4. Score with the rubric and note one strength and one improvement.

#### 0:40–0:45 · Stretch break (5 min, stretch break)
*A five-minute stretch and movement break (seated option available).*

1. Invite everyone to stand, stretch, walk or drink water for five minutes; offer a seated option for anyone who prefers or needs it.
2. Restart on time with a one-line reminder of where the session is and what comes next.

#### 0:45–1:15 · Pitches and questions - block 2 (30 min, assessment and feedback)
*The next six presenters in each room pitch; recorded presenters answer their live questions.*

1. The remaining pitchers in each live room (7 to 12) go now, with the same timer, rubric and question approach as block 1.
2. In a recorded-questions room, do not replay the recordings (the panel scored them before the session); give each recorded presenter 2 minutes of live questions about their business pitch.
3. Each room's panel hands its completed business pitch rubric sheets to the room facilitator before the feedback round.

#### 1:15–1:25 · Feedback round (10 min, group activity)
*Share feedback and learn from patterns across pitches.*

1. Learners give written feedback to their two assigned presenters.
2. Facilitator highlights patterns in strong pitches - real evidence, honest numbers, a clear ask.
3. Model receiving tough feedback calmly - thank, clarify, decide.

#### 1:25–1:40 · Pitching yourself and workplace readiness (15 min, main lesson)
*Use your business project to pitch yourself as an entrepreneur or job candidate.*

1. Entrepreneur self-pitch: 'I am Kevin from Kitui. I test ideas with real customers before spending money. I tested a solar lamp rental with five households; four paid on time and two referred neighbours. I am looking for a partner with a motorbike to expand deliveries.'
2. Employability: the project shows skills employers want - research, numeracy, customer service, sales, presenting and taking initiative. Practise describing it in an interview using situation, action and result.
3. Pathways: starting the business in small steps, joining a small business, employment while building the business on the side, further training; support networks such as county enterprise offices, youth business programmes and DIRA alumni. Beware of 'business opportunities' that ask for joining fees or promise quick returns (pyramid schemes).
4. Professional follow-up: thank guests and test customers within two days; follow up on any offer of help with a short, specific message.
5. Learners practise their self-pitch once in pairs.

#### 1:40–1:55 · 30-day validation action plan (15 min, practical exercise)
*Write your next realistic validation step and a 30-day plan.*

1. Learners complete the 30-day validation plan: the main assumption still to test, the next validation step (for example 'get 20 paying customers', 'run a two-week pilot'), four weekly actions, a measure of success, an accountability partner, one risk and a plan.
2. Learners share their next validation step with their accountability partner.

#### 1:55–2:00 · Celebration and close (5 min, conclusion and assignment)
*Celebrate and learn what happens next.*

1. Celebrate each learner's pitch in one sentence.
2. Explain how to submit the pitch deck, concept summary and 30-day validation action in DIRA LEARN, how results and certificates follow the programme's rules, and how alumni can connect with mentors and youth enterprise funding information.

### 6. Practical demonstrations
- **Model pitch**
    1. Before the session, share the recorded Fresh Fold example pitch as a reference. Do not use showcase time for it.

### 7. Guided exercises
- **Situation, action, result:** Describe your business project in interview style using situation, action and result.

### 8. Individual activities
- **Business pitch:** Deliver your 3-minute pitch with evidence and a specific ask, and answer questions for two minutes.
- **30-day validation plan:** Complete the plan with the next validation step, weekly actions, measure, partner and risk.

### 9. Group activities
- **Peer feedback:** Give rubric-based written feedback to two assigned presenters.
- **Self-pitch practice:** Practise your entrepreneur self-pitch in pairs.

### 10. Real-world examples (Kenya)
- A Kitui solar lamp rental pitch with a five-household test.
- A Nakuru laundry pick-up pitch with 12 pre-orders from hostel students.
- A learner who stopped a juice idea after testing and pitched a changed idea for office snack delivery.

### 11. Tools and resources
- Learners' pitches (slides or one-page visuals), pitch rubric, feedback cards or DIRA LEARN
- Timer and 30-day validation plan template

### 12. Assessment questions and activities
| Question or task | What a good answer shows |
|---|---|
| Pitch scored with the business pitch rubric. | Use rubric descriptors; evidence and honest numbers weigh most. |
| What is your next validation step and how will you measure it? | A specific, realistic step with a number. |

### 13. Expected learner outputs

- Business concept summary (submitted in DIRA LEARN)
- 30-day validation action plan
- Written feedback given to two peers

### 14. Facilitator instructions
**Before the session**
- Publish the pitch order; check slides or visuals work.
- Invite guests (local entrepreneurs, partners) and brief them on respectful questions and safeguarding expectations.
- Confirm the room plan: count the learners in the track group, book the rooms and co-facilitators, brief the peer panels, and publish each room's presenter order and the list of recorded presenters by the end of session 7.

**During the session**
- Keep strict time; protect nervous presenters from harsh questioning.
- Celebrate learners who changed or stopped ideas based on evidence.
- Session 8 room plan - Room plan (set in session 7 from the track group size; the session is never extended): up to 12 learners - one room; 13 to 24 - two rooms in parallel; 25 to 36 - three rooms, or two live rooms plus one recorded-questions room. Each live room takes at most 12 presenters: 6 in each 30-minute block, 3 minutes to present and 2 minutes of questions each.
- Every room has a facilitator or briefed co-facilitator (another trainer, a mentor or DIRA staff member who knows the rubric and the safeguarding expectations) and a peer panel of two learners who present in the other block. All rooms use the same rubric.
- Recorded option: learners in a recorded-questions room, and anyone who cannot present live (absence, connection problems, anxiety, or the privacy option), submit a 3-minute recording - video, or a voice note with slides for low data - by the day before session 8. The panel scores it in advance with the same rubric; in session 8 each recorded presenter answers 2 minutes of live questions (12 per block). If more learners need the recorded option than one room can take, the panel sends written questions and the learner answers them in DIRA LEARN.
- Timekeeping: a visible timer with a 30-second warning; stop at 3 minutes. If a block runs late, cut questions to 1 minute - do not extend the session.

**Inclusion and safeguarding**
- Learners who cannot present live can submit a recorded pitch according to the programme's rules.
- Guests must not make funding or business offers directly to learners outside programme channels; route any offers through DIRA staff.

**After the session**
- Enter rubric scores and feedback in DIRA LEARN grading.
- Record attendance.

### 15. Homework and follow-up
- Submit the pitch, business concept and 30-day validation plan in DIRA LEARN.
- Send thank-you messages to the customers and guests who helped.

### 16. How this differs from other tracks
- Every track ends with the same pitch-yourself and workplace-readiness segment; the Entrepreneurship & Business rubric weights evidence and numbers.
